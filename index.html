About Me

I am completing my PhD in Cognitive Neuroscience at the Ernst Strüngmann Institute of the Max Planck Society, in the Rademaker Lab, Frankfurt am Main, Germany. My research combines psychophysics, functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI), eye tracking, and computational modelling to investigate how the brain represents and maintains visual information in memory, predicts the future states of the world, and supports behavior when sensory information is incomplete.

Before starting my PhD, I graduated with dual degrees in Science & Technology and Neuroscience from the Universidade Federal do ABC, Brazil, where I later completed a Master's degree in Neuroscience and Cognition with Prof. Marcelo Salvador Caetano and Prof. Claudinei Biazoli Jr. in the Timing and Cognition Lab. Before moving to Germany, I worked as a research assistant with Dr. Joana Balardin at Hospital Israelita Albert Einstein, Brazil, and Prof. Jorge Almeida at the University of Coimbra, Portugal. During this time, I contributed to research on visual object recognition, stress-induced changes in brain function, motor development in young children, and the effects of physical exercise on functional connectivity during healthy aging.

Outside the lab, I am usually learning to fly an airplane, cycling, playing the drums, or scuba diving. Whatever I am doing, I am driven by the same curiosity that motivates my research: How does our brain allow us to learn complex tasks and adapt to an ever-changing environment?

Research

Our visual experience of the world extends beyond what is immediately available to our eyes. We remember information that is no longer visible and anticipate what is likely to happen next. By maintaining and predicting visual information, our brain allows us to interact effectively with a dynamic environment.

My research investigates the neural mechanisms that support these internal visual representations. Specifically, how visual information is maintained in memory and modulated by behavioral demands, transformed into predictions of future events, and ultimately used to guide behavior. To answer these questions, I combine psychophysics, functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI), eye tracking, and computational modelling.

01

Visual Working Memory

Could task requirements reshaped the visual representations we hold in memory?

Abstract

Visual working memory (VWM) allows for temporary storage of relevant information to support adaptive behavior. Prior fMRI studies investigating the mechanisms of passive storage have decoded mnemonic information from activity patterns in the early visual cortex (EVC). Given that stored information is ultimately used to accomplish specific tasks, might differences in recall requirements lead to differences in EVC involvement? Here, we manipulated recall requirements during an orientation memory task to investigate how adaptive behavior impacts EVC representations. Specifically, we used four recall conditions to dissociate the roles of visual input, motor output, and online monitoring. To report a remembered orientation, participants (1) used “interactive” button presses to rotate a thin dial on the screen, (2) viewed a “matched replay” of a pre-recorded response and indicated a clockwise or counter-clockwise offset of the final dial orientation, (3) viewed a “mismatched replay”, i.e. a pre-recorded response to a random orientation, and pressed a button if and when the dial crossed the remembered orientation, and (4) used button presses to rotate an “invisible dial” whose initial orientation was shown only briefly before response onset. We uncovered a drastic increase in memory decoding during recall when the remembered orientation matched the final dial orientation (“interactive” and “matched replay” conditions). This implies that visual input alone (a dial rotating), motor output alone (many button presses), or online monitoring alone (how close are memory and dial) cannot account for high memory decoding in EVC during recall. Instead, our findings suggest amplified information in EVC when mnemonic contents match sensory input during recall.

02

Spatiotemporal Prediction

How does the visual system keep track of moving objects when they temporarily disappear from view?

Abstract

Intercepting or avoiding collision with moving objects, such as while driving or playing sports, requires anticipating how object positions change across time and space. This ability is particularly advantageous when objects are temporarily occluded from view, for example when estimating the position of a cyclist passing behind a parked vehicle. How do we predict the location of a moving object that is no longer visible? Different hypotheses share the assumption that information encoded during the visible portion of an object’s trajectory is used to estimate its position, or time to reappearance, in the absence of visual input. In the brain, visual cortex has been shown to represent information about anticipated visual events and to preserve motion-related features during occlusion (e.g. motion direction). However, it remains unclear whether visual cortex continuously updates the representation of an object’s location during multiple seconds of occlusion. Here, we used fMRI to test whether visual cortex supports sustained spatial tracking during occluded motion and whether such representations are modulated by attention. Participants viewed a target moving along a circular trajectory under one of three conditions: They had to discriminate the location of the target while the trajectory was (1) fully visible, (2) half-occluded, or (3) they performed a task at fixation while the target trajectory was half-occluded. We found that representations of the occluded object’s location were continuously updated in visual cortex throughout occlusion periods lasting up to four seconds. Diverting attention away from the moving stimulus attenuated, but did not eliminate, these spatial representations. Moreover, the strength of early visual cortex representations predicted behavioral uncertainty when estimating object location under occlusion. Together, these findings support a largely automatic tracking account of motion extrapolation, demonstrating that visual cortex dynamically updates spatial information about attended and unattended objects that are temporarily out of sight.

03

Motion & Memory

How does the nature of a motion signal affect the precision with which it is remembered?

Abstract

Our daily visual environment is abound with motion. Accurately perceiving object speed is essential when avoiding collisions or intercepting moving objects. Accurately remembering object speed is equally essential, as moving objects may become temporarily occluded. Most studies of speed use texture motion stimuli (e.g., dot motion) which minimize spatial cues, but do not reflect the natural spatiotemporal dynamics of moving objects. In contrast, object motion contains rich spatial cues, better capturing real-world motion. Neural mechanisms underlying texture and object motion processing differ across the visual hierarchy. Might memory for speed also differ between texture and object motion? To explore this question, we tested human participants on speed recall for both texture and object motion stimuli across different speeds (2–32 º/s) and delays (1–8 s). Both types of target stimuli moved along a circular trajectory (for 4–6 s) and were recalled via method-of-adjustment. Replicating previous work, we found a strong decrease in speed recall performance with increasing target speed. Importantly, we also observed that recall was significantly worse for texture compared to object motion stimuli, implying an advantage for spatiotemporally bound objects. For object motion, longer target and delay durations led to better and worse speed recall, respectively. Gaze was subtly biased toward the location of the object moving along its trajectory. For both types of stimuli, responses were biased toward the recall probe, and congruency between target and probe direction improved speed recall. Together, our findings provide a solid psychophysical basis for understanding human short-term memory for speed.

04

Computational Models

What are computational mechanisms underlying spatiotemporal prediction in the visual system?

Abstract

Estimating the location of a moving object during occlusion requires the brain to make predictions in absence of corresponding sensory input. Behavioral studies consistently report that the longer an object remains hidden, the greater is the uncertainty in estimating the object’s location. However, the computational mechanisms underlying this temporal decay remain unclear. Predictive coding theory proposes that higher-level brain regions generate predictions that propagate to early sensory areas via feedback, where they are compared against incoming input. However, predictive signals can be observed in the early visual cortex even when attention is diverted away from a target, or when subjects are anesthetized. This raises the question whether feedback from higher-level areas is necessary, or whether local recurrent circuits could facilitate spatiotemporal predictions. To test this, we trained three convolutional neural network architectures on a video-frame prediction task. The networks differed in the pathways through which information flowed within the different layers, simulating feedforward, recurrent, and feedback processes. We then compared human and model performance on a task where a single dot was rotating around a half-occluded circular trajectory. In humans, the precision with which the dot was located decayed with increasing occlusion duration, replicating previous findings. Location estimates led the true position early during occlusion, and lagged behind later, indicating a bias in responses. All network architectures also exhibited decay when predicting frames autoregressively, with feedforward models declining most steeply, followed by recurrent and feedback models. The recurrent model most closely mirrored the decay observed in humans. These findings suggest that local recurrent dynamics may play a role in sustaining spatiotemporal predictions over perceptual gaps.

A collection of side projects exploring vision science outside the traditional laboratory settings: from natural image datasets, to cockpit eye tracking, to playful MRI demonstrations.

Field Note 01

FiSH & CHiPs Dataset

Together with Amit Rawal, I was awarded the Tom Troscianko Grant to attend ECVP 2024, where we created an image dataset from landscapes photographed across Belgium, France, England, and Scotland.

Together with Naveen Balachandran, the dataset has been curated and preprocessed, and will shortly be made publicly available for the vision science community.

Visual Scanning in the Cockpit

While learning how to fly, I recorded my own eye movements in the cockpit using wearable eye tracking glasses (featured by Indivisual Lab ), capturing my erratic gaze patterns between flight instruments, the outside view, maps, and the runway.
